## The flagged disagreement

This is a real, clearly-documented split, not a manufactured one.

**Side one (dominant by volume):** Derek Prince's extensive Isaiah 53/
atonement material states directly that healing is fully provided already,
that "the Bible never puts healing in the future," and that the operative
question is not whether God is willing but how a person appropriates what's
already been given. This places the variable on the human side.

**Side two (thin by volume, but a real named voice on the exact question):**
Jack Deere's "Responding to Common Questions About Healing" document — his
only real contribution to this corpus on this topic — argues explicitly that
healing is not guaranteed in this life, that it is not helpful or accurate
to tell someone they weren't healed for lack of faith, and cites Paul
leaving Trophimus sick (2 Timothy 4:20) and instructing Timothy toward
ordinary remedies (1 Timothy 5:23) as scriptural evidence that even the
apostles didn't see uniform healing. Daniel Kolenda is a second real, independent voice here, not just a minor
one-line echo — a newly reviewed source (a podcast/interview transcript,
outside the existing database corpus) shows him articulating this same
position at length and in his own words: healing is not guaranteed, that
sometimes faith simply isn't the explanation when it doesn't happen, and
that he has personally prayed for people who recovered and people who
didn't without claiming to know why. That's a substantially stronger
second voice on this side than the single in-corpus proposition alone
suggested. Zac Poonen's thorn-in-the-flesh material (2 Corinthians 12:7)
adds a third, slightly different angle — God sometimes withholding healing
on purpose, for sanctifying reasons, rather than the absence just being
unexplained.

**Why I didn't resolve it:** these aren't reconcilable with a soft "both/
and" sentence without actually taking a side — Prince's framing puts the
explanatory weight on human appropriation when healing doesn't come; Deere's
framing explicitly rejects that as the default explanation. That's a real
theological fork (common across the wider faith-healing/charismatic
tradition, not unique to this corpus), and it showed up here with real named
voices on both sides, so I left it as the paper's one explicit editorial
placeholder rather than picking a side or writing around it.

## Thin-coverage spots, stated honestly

- **Gifts of healing as a distinct spiritual gift** (1 Corinthians 12:9,
  plural "gifts," possibly implying different people carry different kinds
  of healing grace) — barely touched independently in this cluster. I
  mentioned it because it's biblically real and closely related, but did
  not build a section around it; there isn't enough here to do so honestly.
  **Partially addressed now** by the same newly reviewed Kolenda source
  used above — it also supplies the assignment-not-at-will framing (the
  gift as a specific, moment-by-moment commissioning rather than a
  standing, on-demand power), which is now reflected in the draft. Still
  thin as an independently developed topic overall, but no longer
  completely unaddressed.
- **Corporate/mass healing ministry** (crusades, healing lines) — Daniel
  Kolenda is a real-world healing evangelist and this would be his natural
  territory, but only 8 propositions from him matched at all, and none
  focus on the practice of large-scale healing ministry specifically — his
  matches here are more general. If Rhemata wants a section on that
  practice specifically, this corpus doesn't currently support it.
