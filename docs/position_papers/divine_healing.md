# Divine Healing
*A Theological Position Paper for Rhemata*

---

## What It Is

Divine healing is God restoring a human body from sickness, injury, or disease by His own direct power — not primarily through medicine, though He can and does work through it, but as a supernatural act flowing from His character and from what Christ accomplished on the cross.

This is not a fringe theme. From Exodus to the Gospels to the early church, healing runs through Scripture as a consistent expression of who God is. Long before the cross, God introduced Himself to His people by this name: "I am the LORD who heals you" (Exodus 15:26). Healing is not an occasional favor God grants the unusually deserving. It is bound up in His own identity.

---

## The Cross Is the Foundation

Healing is not treated in Scripture as a separate blessing loosely connected to salvation. It is anchored in the same atoning work that deals with sin.

- **Isaiah 53:4-5** — "Surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows... and with his wounds we are healed." The prophecy of the cross names sickness and sin together, in the same breath, as things Christ carried.
- **Matthew 8:16-17** — the Gospel writer explicitly connects Jesus' healing ministry to that same prophecy: "This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah: 'He took our illnesses and bore our diseases.'"
- **1 Peter 2:24** — "By his wounds you have been healed." Peter, writing after the resurrection, states it as an already-accomplished fact, not a future hope.

This is why healing is not approached as a request into the dark, hoping God might be willing. The cross already answered the question of God's willingness. What it purchased, it purchased for the whole person — spirit, soul, and body.

---

## Jesus Made Healing a Normal Part of His Ministry

Healing was never incidental to what Jesus did. Acts summarizes His entire earthly work this way: He "went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him" (Acts 10:38). Sickness in Scripture is treated as an intrusion into what God originally made good, and Jesus moved against it everywhere He went, healing "all who were sick" who came to Him (Matthew 8:16), not a select few.

And He did not reserve this for Himself. Before ascending, He told His followers plainly that believers would lay hands on the sick and see them recover (Mark 16:17-18). James, writing to the church, gives a direct instruction for exactly this: call the elders, anoint with oil, pray in faith, and "the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick" (James 5:14-15). Healing was handed to the church as an ongoing practice, not preserved as a memory of what Jesus alone once did.

---

## How Healing Comes

Scripture describes more than one channel through which healing flows, not a single required method:

- **The prayer of faith** — James 5:14-15, prayed over a sick believer, often with the involvement of church leadership, not necessarily alone.
- **The laying on of hands** — a simple, physical act of faith carrying real spiritual weight (Mark 16:18, Luke 4:40).
- **The spoken word** — Jesus regularly healed by command, not only by touch. Scripture also describes God's own word as carrying healing power in itself: "my words... are life to those who find them, and healing to all their flesh" (Proverbs 4:20-22).
- **Spiritual gifts** — healing is explicitly listed among the gifts the Spirit distributes to the church (1 Corinthians 12:9), meaning some believers carry a recurring grace for it beyond what is available to every believer in an ordinary moment of prayer.

The gift of healing (1 Corinthians 12:9) is not a claim to heal at will. Scripture presents it as a specific assignment given for a specific moment, not a permanent, guaranteed power a person carries and dispenses on command. Collapsing that distinction either overstates what any healing minister actually claims, or gives critics grounds to define the gift out of existence by holding it to a standard Scripture never set in the first place.

None of these are formulas that force an outcome. They are the means God has given; what happens through them remains His to give.

## An unresolved tension worth naming honestly

**[EDITORIAL DECISION NEEDED — ALEX]** There is a real difference of emphasis running through the teaching behind this paper on one specific point, and it should not be flattened into false agreement. One strand teaches that healing is fully and unconditionally provided in the atonement already — that the real question is never "is it God's will to heal me," since that was settled at the cross, but "how do I appropriate what has already been provided," placing the working variable on the believer's side (faith, confession, persistence). Another strand teaches just as directly that healing is not guaranteed in this life for everyone who asks, that unbelief is not the only explanation when healing doesn't come, and specifically warns against telling someone who wasn't healed that they simply didn't have enough faith — pointing to cases like Paul leaving a co-worker sick (2 Timothy 4:20) as evidence that even the apostles didn't see every prayer for healing answered the same way. Both positions are represented by real conviction, not haste, and this paper does not adjudicate between "healing withheld means something is wrong with your faith" and "healing withheld can simply be a mystery held in God's hands." Decide how directly this paper should state its own position before this section is finalized.

## Common Objections

**"If healing was fully provided at the cross, why doesn't everyone who prays get healed?"**
This is precisely the tension named above, and this paper is not going to paper over it with a tidy answer it doesn't actually have settled. What can be said without controversy: the cross secured healing as a real, present provision, not a bare future hope — but exactly how that provision meets an individual's particular circumstance is not always simple or immediate, and pressuring someone toward guilt over an unanswered prayer is never the right response, whichever way this gets settled. The objection that a real healing gift should mean visiting a hospital and healing every patient in it sounds forceful but assumes something Scripture never promises: that access, consent, and outcome are all guaranteed the moment healing is offered. None of the three are. Even Jesus did not heal everyone at the pool of Bethesda.

**"Isn't this just a promise of physical comfort with no place for actual suffering?"**
No. Scripture makes room for suffering that is not immediately removed, without ever treating sickness itself as something to be quietly accepted as good. Praying for healing and walking with someone through unresolved suffering are not opposites — both take seriously that God is present and good in the middle of a body that is not yet whole.

**"Doesn't this replace real medicine?"**
No. Nothing here requires refusing a doctor while waiting on God, and doing both is not a failure of faith. God works through means as freely as He works apart from them; the two are not in competition.

---

## What This Is Not Saying

This is not a claim that sickness is always caused by personal sin or a specific lack of faith. Scripture nowhere makes that a universal rule, and using it that way turns a suffering person's pain into an accusation against them.

This is not a claim that healing prayer is a transaction with a guaranteed mechanical outcome — say the right words, believe hard enough, and recovery is owed. Healing flows from a Person, not a formula.

This is not a dismissal of medicine, doctors, or ordinary physical care as somehow evidence of weak faith. Praying for supernatural healing and pursuing wise medical treatment are not in tension.

And this is not a claim that every prayer for healing will be answered visibly or on the timeline that is hoped for. What is claimed is that God's character and Christ's finished work make Him a God who heals — not that every specific request resolves the way it is asked.

---

## Living in Light of It

The point of this teaching was never to produce anxiety over whether enough faith has been mustered. It is to establish, plainly, that sickness is not something God is indifferent to, and that praying for healing — for yourself and for others — is not a long shot or a last resort. It is a normal, expected part of following a God who has always identified Himself as the One who heals.
