# Prosperity and Faith Teaching
*A Theological Position Paper for Rhemata*

---

## What It Is

God is not indifferent to whether His people have what they need. He is a Father, and Scripture never treats a believer's material need as more spiritually impressive than a believer's material provision. "Beloved, I pray that all may go well with you and that you may be in good health, as it goes well with your soul" (3 John 2) is a real prayer for a real friend's real circumstances, not a metaphor. Faith, in this teaching, is simply taking God at His word about His own character — that He is good, that He is a provider, and that He can be trusted with ordinary, practical needs, not only spiritual ones.

This is not the same claim as "God wants everyone wealthy." It is the much narrower, much steadier claim that God's care for His people is not confined to the spiritual and unseen — it reaches their actual circumstances, and trusting Him with those circumstances is not a lesser, less spiritual kind of faith.

---

## God Provides, and Generosity Is How His People Live

Scripture roots material provision in God's own character, not in a technique for extracting it from Him. "My God will supply every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19). Provision flows from who God is and what Christ has already done — "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor, so that you by his poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9) — not from a formula that obligates Him.

Because of this, giving is treated in Scripture as worship, not as an investment strategy: "each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver" (2 Corinthians 9:7). Giving generously — including tithing, a practice that predates the law itself, going back to Abraham's own example — is a normal expression of trust in God as provider. It is never described as a transaction that purchases His blessing.

## An unresolved tension worth naming honestly

**[EDITORIAL DECISION NEEDED — ALEX]** The teaching behind this paper does not agree on how much material, financial increase a believer should actually expect as a normal outcome of faith and obedience — and this paper is not going to manufacture agreement that isn't there. One thread affirms real financial and material blessing as a genuine part of what the cross secured, describes multiple levels of increasing prosperity as a normal trajectory for a believer walking with God, and treats material need itself as something the enemy has stolen and God intends to restore — while still explicitly warning against making wealth into an idol or treating it as proof of righteousness. Another thread pushes considerably harder in the opposite direction — teaching that believers should stop expecting or longing for the kind of material increase promised under the old covenant, that chasing prosperity and comfort has replaced sacrifice as the church's actual religion, and that true riches are almost entirely reframed as spiritual: Christlike character, the Spirit's presence, eternal reward rather than earthly increase. Both threads reject the crude "wealth proves faith" formula outright — that much is common ground. What is not common ground is how much material increase this teaching should actually lead someone to expect, ask for, or measure their faith by. This paper does not settle that question.

## Common Objections

**"Isn't 'prosperity teaching' just a way to get people to give more money?"**
Where it's used that way, it's a real and serious abuse — Scripture itself has sharp words for people who treat godliness as "a means of gain" (1 Timothy 6:5). But the abuse of a real thing doesn't erase the real thing. God's provision for His people, and the call to give generously in response to His character, are both genuinely biblical; a manipulative use of either does not make either one false.

**"Doesn't this ignore all the suffering, poor, and persecuted believers throughout history and today?"**
It should never be taught in a way that does. Scripture is honest that the wicked sometimes prosper and the righteous sometimes suffer (Job 21, Ecclesiastes), and nowhere claims a straight, guaranteed line from faith to financial ease. Whatever this paper says about God's goodness as provider, it does not say that every faithful believer will be materially comfortable, or that a suffering believer's faith is therefore in question.

**"Isn't wanting to be wealthy just greed with religious language attached?"**
It can be, which is exactly why Scripture puts a real warning next to its real promises: "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils" (1 Timothy 6:10), and "life does not consist in the abundance of one's possessions" (Luke 12:15). Wanting provision is not the same as loving money. The difference is what's actually being trusted, and what's actually being sought first.

---

## What This Is Not Saying

This is not a promise that faith guarantees health and wealth. Scripture explicitly rejects that formula in its own pages — Job's friends preached exactly this, insisting his suffering proved hidden sin, and God directly rebuked them for it (Job 42:7-8). Faithful people suffer. Unfaithful people sometimes prosper. Neither proves anything about the other.

This is not a claim that material prosperity is evidence of righteousness, spiritual maturity, or God's favor — and its absence is not evidence of sin, weak faith, or God's disfavor either. Both errors run in the same direction: turning a bank balance into a verdict on someone's standing with God, which Scripture refuses to do.

This is not permission for manipulative giving appeals — the pressure to give in order to unlock a financial breakthrough, the promise of guaranteed material return on a gift, or the targeting of people who can least afford it with the heaviest appeals. Giving that flows from fear, guilt, or a transactional promise has already lost what makes it giving in the first place.

This is not a claim that poverty is itself righteous or that wealth is itself sinful. Scripture treats riches as morally neutral in themselves — what matters is whether they are trusted, hoarded, and idolized, or held loosely and used well.

And this is not a claim that every believer's material circumstances reflect the true state of their faith. Circumstances are not a scoreboard. They never were.

---

## Living in Light of It

Whatever the unresolved question above, the settled ground under all of it does not move: God is good, He is a provider, and trusting Him with real, practical needs is not a lesser kind of faith than trusting Him with spiritual ones. Give generously because that is who He is. Trust Him with what you actually need. And hold every circumstance — plenty or lack — with an open hand, because neither one is the real measure of anything that actually matters.
